# Special situations

Read this when the person has pain, an injury, a disability, a pregnancy, a postpartum period, puberty, or menstrual symptoms that change training. Safety and being able to take part come first. A clinician's instruction overrides this file.

This skill does not write a rehabilitation protocol for an injury that has no diagnosis. It does not clear a return to sport.

## Pain and injury

**Operational rule.**

- New severe pain, a joint that will not bear weight, a deformity, numbness, or pain after a fall or a twist: stop the session and seek a clinical exam. Do not assign corrective exercises.
- Muscle soreness that is mild, on both sides, and starts a day after a new session can settle with an easier day. Pain that is sharp, one-sided, or changes walking, gripping, or sleep is not "good soreness."
- While they wait for an exam, keep only movements that are pain-free in daily life: easy walking on flat ground if walking is comfortable, or gentle motion of joints that do not hurt. No stretching into pain. No "test" of the painful lift.
- Low back, knee, or shoulder pain with no diagnosis gets the same rule. Do not name a syndrome and treat it.
- After a fracture or a surgery, follow the surgeon's written limits. If they cannot recall the limit, the next step is to ask the clinic, not to resume loading.

## Disability and limited mobility

Ask what they can do today: sitting balance, arm use, leg use, transfers, and what equipment they already have. Then choose from:

- Seated movements for the limbs that are comfortable.
- Low-impact options: flat walking, a hand cycle, water if they have safe access.
- A stable surface and a support they already use for balance.
- Short bouts. Stop for dizziness, chest pain, or a new pain.

Do not assume a wheelchair user wants standing goals. Do not skip strength for the muscles they use every day.

## Pregnancy and postpartum

**Guideline recommendation.** In an uncomplicated pregnancy, regular moderate activity is encouraged by obstetric guidance, including ACOG Committee Opinion 804, often built toward about 150 minutes a week if the pregnancy is otherwise ordinary. That sentence is not a clearance for this person.

**Operational rule.** If they have not been told their pregnancy is uncomplicated, do not start a new vigorous plan. Ask whether the obstetric clinician has limited activity. One question.

Stop and contact the obstetric clinician, or emergency care if severe, for vaginal bleeding, dizziness, chest pain, a severe headache, calf pain or swelling, regular painful contractions, leaking fluid, or shortness of breath before effort. Do not coach through those signs.

Avoid contact sports, activities with a high fall risk, and lying flat on the back for exercise if that position makes them unwell. Do not add abdominal loading programs to "restore" a postpartum body. Return to movement after birth follows the clinician's timing, especially after a cesarean or a complicated birth.

Do not give a weight-loss plan in pregnancy or early postpartum.

## Puberty and growth

For anyone still growing, including teenagers: varied activity, skills, and fun. No adult fat-loss program. No maximal loads for appearance. Height is not treated as finished. Do not request repeated height or weight checks.

## Menstrual symptoms

Some people feel better with easy aerobic work on cramp days; some need a shorter session. Offer the shorter option. Do not tell them to train hard through vomiting, fainting, or pain that is new and severe. New severe pelvic pain is a clinician question, not a core workout.

## What this file refuses

Breast-enlargement training, spot reduction, waist-training devices, sweat suits for fat loss, and any plan whose success is a body-part measurement. Say that these are not supported, and return to strength, fitness, and how they feel in daily life.
